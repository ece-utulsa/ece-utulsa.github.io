Parallel Plate Capacitor

Interactive exploration of capacitor physics, charging behavior, and energy storage

What Is a Capacitor?

A capacitor is a passive two-terminal device that stores energy in an electric field between two conductive plates separated by a dielectric (insulating) material. When voltage is applied across the plates, positive charge accumulates on one plate and negative charge on the other, creating an electric field in the gap.

This simulation models a parallel plate capacitor connected to a voltage source through a resistor (an RC circuit). You can observe how the capacitor charges and discharges, how current flows, and how energy is stored — all in real time.

Key Equations

Capacitance from Geometry

The capacitance of a parallel plate capacitor depends on its physical construction:

C = κ ε₀ A / d
κ = dielectric constant · ε₀ = 8.854 × 10−12 F/m · A = plate area · d = plate spacing

Increasing the plate area or dielectric constant increases capacitance. Increasing the plate spacing decreases it. In this simulation, three geometry sliders directly determine the circuit capacitance — there is no separate capacitance knob.

Voltage–Current Relationship

The fundamental capacitor equation — current is proportional to the rate of change of voltage:

i(t) = C · dv/dt
Current flows only when the voltage is changing

This is why DC voltage produces zero current at steady state — the voltage is not changing. With AC waveforms, the continuously changing voltage produces continuous current.

RC Circuit Time Constant

When a resistor is present, the RC time constant governs how fast the capacitor charges and discharges:

τ = R · C
After 5τ, the capacitor is approximately 99.3% charged

Energy Storage

The energy stored in the capacitor's electric field:

E = ½ C v²
Energy depends on the square of the voltage

Power

P = v · i
Positive = storing energy · Negative = releasing energy

What the Simulation Shows

Center Canvas — Capacitor Cross-Section

The animated cross-section shows the physical capacitor in operation:

  • Plates — dark bars with charge symbols (+/−) that intensify as the capacitor charges
  • Electric field lines — dashed lines between the plates showing E-field direction and strength
  • Dielectric polarization — when κ > 1, dipoles appear showing how the dielectric material responds to the electric field
  • Current arrow (I) — direction and magnitude of circuit current
  • Voltage annotation (V) — polarity and magnitude across the capacitor
  • Plate geometry — plate width and gap change as you adjust Area and Spacing sliders

Plot Grid — Four Time-Domain Plots

  • Voltage (V) — capacitor voltage over time
  • Current (mA) — circuit current over time
  • Power (mW) — instantaneous power
  • Energy (μJ) — stored energy over time

Suggested Experiments

Six guided explorations to try once you launch the simulation

Experiment 1
RC Charging Curve
Select DC Step. Set R = 1000 Ω. Press Play and observe the exponential voltage rise and current decay. The time constant τ = RC is displayed in the sidebar Info section.
→ Try doubling R or changing geometry to double C — watch how τ changes and how the charging speed responds.
Experiment 2
Current Leads Voltage
Select Sine waveform with R = 0. Watch the voltage and current plots carefully.
→ The current waveform leads voltage by exactly 90°. Current peaks when voltage is changing fastest (at zero crossings). This is i = C · dv/dt in action.
Experiment 3
Dielectric Effect
With a Sine waveform running, slowly increase κ from 1 to 10.
→ Watch dipoles appear between the plates, the C value increase, and current amplitude grow — more capacitance means more charge flows for the same voltage change.
Experiment 4
Geometry and Capacitance
With any AC waveform, increase the plate area (A) — plates widen, C increases, current increases. Then increase spacing (d) — gap widens, C decreases, current decreases.
→ See C = κε₀A/d in action both visually and electrically. The geometry directly drives the circuit behavior.
Experiment 5
Square Wave Current Spikes
Select Square waveform with R = 0. The voltage switches instantly between +V₀ and −V₀.
→ Since i = C · dv/dt, instantaneous voltage changes produce large current spikes. Now add resistance (R > 0) and the spikes become finite exponential transients.
Experiment 6
Energy Storage and Release
Select Sine and watch the Energy plot. Since E = ½Cv², energy peaks when |v| is maximum and drops to zero at v = 0.
→ The Power plot shows positive values (storing) and negative values (releasing). A capacitor alternately absorbs and returns energy each half-cycle — it does not dissipate energy like a resistor.
