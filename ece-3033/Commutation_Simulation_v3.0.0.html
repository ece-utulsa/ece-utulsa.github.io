What commutation actually does

A motor needs every conductor under the N pole to carry current one way and every conductor under the S pole to carry it the other way, no matter which piece of copper happens to be there. The coil keeps moving from one pole to the other, so its current has to be reversed every half turn. The commutator is the switch that does that reversing, and it is turned by the shaft itself.

1. The force on one wire

F = I L × B   →   reverse I or reverse B and the force reverses

Here the field points down, from the N pole (top) to the S pole (bottom). A wire under N carrying current out of the page (•) is pushed right. A wire under S carrying current into the page (×) is pushed left. On opposite sides of the shaft, those two pushes both turn the rotor clockwise.

2. Why a plain loop fails (mode ①)

Connect the loop to the supply through two full slip rings, and each end of the loop is always wired to the same terminal. Side 1 always carries current out of the page. That is fine while side 1 is under N, but half a turn later side 1 is under S, with the same current in a reversed field, so the force reverses. The torque changes sign every half turn, and on DC the rotor just rocks back and forth and settles at the neutral line. Run mode ① with “Own torque” and watch it fail.

3. Split the ring (mode ②)

Cut the ring into two halves, segments a and b, insulated from each other. The halves turn with the loop, and the brushes stay put. So whichever half is currently on the right touches the + brush, whatever end of the loop it belongs to. Each time the loop passes the horizontal (neutral) line, the gap passes under the brushes and the connections swap:

The current in the loop reversed. The current in space (• under N, × under S) did not. That is the whole idea.

4. When the switch happens, and why there

The brush is a little wider than the insulating gap, so for a moment it touches both segments. The coil is then shorted through the brush, and its current runs down through zero and builds up the other way (shown in magenta). The segments are positioned so that this happens exactly when the coil sides are in the neutral zone between the poles, where B ≈ 0. The coil produces no torque there anyway, so it can reverse without ever pushing the wrong way. If the brushes were rotated away from the neutral line, the switch would happen under a pole and the coil would briefly fight the motor (and spark).

5. Two points of view

Sitting on the rotor, the coil sees an alternating current: a square wave that flips every half turn (top graph). Sitting with the brushes, the supply sees a steady DC current (middle graph). The commutator is a mechanical reversing switch, a rectifier or inverter depending on which way the power flows, synchronized to the rotor because it is built onto the rotor.

6. Why real machines use many coils (mode ③)

One loop gives a torque that drops to zero twice per turn, and if the rotor stops in that dead spot it cannot restart. With four coils and four segments (a–d), the coils are connected end to end in a closed ring. The brushes split that ring into two parallel paths, each carrying half the current. Only the coils passing the neutral line are being commutated at any moment, while the others sit under the poles at full current, so the torque never reaches zero. Pick a coil with Follow one coil and you’ll see its current flip every half turn, while the • / × pattern on the screen never changes.

Model notes

  • Radial air-gap field: full strength under the pole faces, tapering to zero in a ±10° neutral zone.
  • The supply is treated as a constant current, I, so the graphs show commutation alone (no back-EMF or armature reaction).
  • Coil currents come from solving the actual circuit at each angle (brush contact resistance ∝ 1/contact area, the classic “resistance commutation” model). That is why the reversal is a ramp rather than an instant flip.
  • The 4-coil machine is the 2-pole lap winding from the course notes: four slots, two coil sides per slot, coil k joined between segments k and k+1.
