Commutation, step by step

A motor needs every conductor under the N pole to carry current one way and every conductor under the S pole to carry it the other way, no matter which piece of copper happens to be there. The coil keeps moving from one pole to the other, so its current has to be reversed every half turn. The commutator is the switch that does that reversing, and it is turned by the shaft itself.

1. The force on one wire

F = I L × B   →   |F| = B I L when the wire is perpendicular to the field

Throughout this tool you look along the shaft, so the wires run into and out of the page. The field points down, from the N pole (top) through the rotor to the S pole (bottom). A wire under N carrying current out of the page (•) is pushed right. A wire under S carrying current into the page (×) is pushed left. Those two pushes are on opposite sides of the shaft, so they both turn the rotor clockwise.

2. From force to torque

Each side of the loop sits at radius r from the shaft and is pushed along the tangent, so it contributes a torque F r. A single loop has two active sides, so:

τ = 2 F r = 2 B I L r   (both sides under a pole face)

Real machines have a narrow radial air gap. The field crosses it at right angles to the rotor surface and is nearly constant under each pole face. The torque is therefore flat while the sides are under the poles, and it drops to zero in the neutral zone between poles, where B ≈ 0. (Flat poles with a uniform field would make the torque vary as a sine of the rotor angle instead. The simulation models the radial gap.)

Worked example

B = 0.5 T, I = 2 A, active length L = 0.10 m, radius r = 0.04 m.
Force on each side: F = B I L = 0.5 × 2 × 0.10 = 0.10 N.
Torque on the loop: τ = 2 F r = 2 × 0.10 × 0.04 = 0.008 N·m. That is tiny, which is why real armatures use many turns in many slots.

3. Why a plain loop fails (machine ①)

Connect the loop to the supply through two full slip rings, and each end of the loop is always wired to the same terminal. Side 1 always carries current out of the page. That is fine while side 1 is under N. Half a turn later, though, side 1 is under S, with the same current in a reversed field, so the force reverses. The torque changes sign every half turn, and on DC the rotor rocks back and forth and settles at the neutral line.

4. Split the ring (machine ②)

Cut the ring into two halves, segments a and b, insulated from each other. The halves turn with the loop, and the brushes stay put. So whichever half is currently on the right touches the + brush, whichever end of the loop it belongs to. Each time the loop passes the horizontal (neutral) line, the gap passes under the brushes and the connections swap:

5. Inside the switch-over

The brush is a little wider than the insulating gap, so for a moment it touches both segments. The coil is then shorted through the brush, and its current runs down through zero and builds up the other way (shown in magenta). This is commutation. In this model the brush spans 18° of the commutator and the gap 3°, so the coil is shorted for about 15° of rotation each time.

Where this happens matters. The segments are positioned so that the switch occurs while the coil sides are in the neutral zone, where B ≈ 0. The coil produces no torque there anyway. Just as important, no voltage is induced in it there (e = B L v ≈ 0), so shorting it drives no large circulating current. If the brushes were rotated away from the neutral line, the switch would happen under a pole: the shorted coil would briefly fight the motor, and the brush would spark. In real machines, armature reaction shifts the neutral plane under load. Large machines add interpoles or compensating windings to keep commutation clean.

6. AC or DC? It depends where you stand

Sitting on the rotor, the coil sees an alternating current: a square-ish wave that flips every time a coil side passes from one pole to the next. Sitting with the brushes, the supply sees a steady DC current. The commutator is a mechanical reversing switch, synchronized to the rotor because it is built onto the rotor. In a motor it acts as an inverter (DC in, AC to the coils). In a generator it acts as a rectifier (AC from the coils, DC out).

fcoil = P n / 120   (P poles, n in r/min)

A 2-pole machine at 1800 r/min puts 30 Hz AC in every armature coil, even though nothing outside the machine is AC.

7. Why real machines use many coils (machine ③)

One loop gives a torque that drops to zero twice per turn. If the rotor stops in that dead spot, it cannot restart. With four coils and four segments (a–d), the coils are connected end to end in a closed ring. The brushes split that ring into two parallel paths, each carrying half the current. Only the coils passing the neutral line are being commutated at any moment, while the others sit under the poles at full current, so the torque never reaches zero. In this model it stays between half and full torque.

This is a 2-pole lap winding. A lap winding has as many parallel paths as poles (a = P), so each conductor carries IA/a. Adding more coils and segments shrinks the ripple further. A real armature with dozens of segments gives a torque that is practically constant.

8. Connecting to the DC machine equations

Add up the torque of every conductor under a pole. With Z conductors in total, each carrying IA/a, each at radius r in field B over length L:

τ = Z (IA/a) B L r

The flux per pole is the field times the pole’s share of the rotor surface, φ = B (2π r L / P), so B L r = φ P / (2π). Substituting:

τind = K φ IA    EA = K φ ω    K = Z P / (2π a)

These are the two equations that the rest of the DC machine course is built on. Commutation is what lets each conductor’s contribution add instead of cancel. To see them at work, open the DC Motor Power-Flow & Performance Lab on the ECE 3033 tools page.

Check yourself

1. You reverse both the supply and the field. Which way does the motor turn?

The same way as before. F = I L × B changes sign once for I and once for B, so the force, and the torque, are unchanged. To reverse a DC motor you reverse one of them, usually the armature.

2. A 2-pole motor runs at 1200 r/min. What is the frequency of the current in one armature coil?

f = P n / 120 = 2 × 1200 / 120 = 20 Hz.

3. Why put the brushes where commutation happens in the neutral zone?

There B ≈ 0, so the shorted coil makes no torque (none is lost) and has no EMF induced in it (no large circulating current, no sparking). Under a pole it would briefly push the wrong way and spark at the brush.

4. A 4-pole lap-wound armature has Z = 360 conductors, φ = 0.02 Wb per pole and IA = 50 A. Find the torque, and EA at 1500 r/min.

Lap: a = P = 4, so K = Z P / (2π a) = 360 × 4 / (2π × 4) = 57.3.
τ = K φ IA = 57.3 × 0.02 × 50 = 57.3 N·m.
ω = 1500 × 2π / 60 = 157.1 rad/s, so EA = K φ ω = 57.3 × 0.02 × 157.1 = 180 V.

Model notes

  • Radial air-gap field: full strength under the pole faces, tapering to zero in a ±10° neutral zone.
  • The supply is treated as a constant current, I, so the graphs show commutation alone (no back-EMF or armature reaction).
  • Coil currents come from solving the actual circuit at each angle (brush contact resistance ∝ 1/contact area, the classic “resistance commutation” model). That is why the reversal is a ramp rather than an instant flip.
  • The 4-coil machine is the 2-pole lap winding from the course notes: four slots, two coil sides per slot, coil k joined between segments k and k+1.

Experiments

Seven short investigations with the simulator · about 45 minutes in all

Each Set up button opens the simulator in the right state. Use ↩ Back (top right of the simulator) to return here. The Rotor angle ψ readout and the graph’s x-axis both measure degrees turned in the motor’s direction. You can drag across the graph to jump to any angle.

Experiment 1
Slip rings: the torque reverses

Show that a loop fed through slip rings gets torque that changes direction every half turn.

Procedure

  1. Starting at ψ = 0°, press 45° ▶ one step at a time.
  2. At each angle, read the end view and the Right now card, and fill in the table.

Questions

  1. How many times per revolution does the torque change direction? Why?
  2. Choose Own torque and press Run. Describe the motion. Where does the rotor come to rest, and why there?
What you should see

Side 1 always carries • (slip rings never swap the ends). It is in the neutral zone at 0° and 180°, under S from 45° to 135° and under N from 225° to 315°. The torque is 0 at 0° and 180°, CCW (backwards) from 45° to 135°, and CW from 225° to 315°. It reverses twice per revolution, each time a side changes poles with the same current. Left on its own, the rotor rocks and settles with the loop horizontal near ψ = 0°, inside the neutral zone, where the torque is zero and any small displacement is pushed back.

Experiment 2
Catch the commutation instant

Find where the split ring reverses the coil current, and what the brushes are doing at that moment.

Procedure

  1. Stop at each commutation is on. Note which segment is on the + brush, then press Run.
  2. When the rotor freezes, fill in a row. Press Run again for the next stop.

Questions

  1. At what angles does commutation happen? Where are the coil sides then, relative to the poles?
  2. Why is it important that the switch happens there and not under a pole?
What you should see

Before the first stop the + brush is on a and the coil carries +I. The rotor freezes at ψ = 0°, then 180°. At each stop the + brush reads a + b (it bridges the gap), the coil current is 0, and the badge reads COMMUTATING. After the first stop the + brush is on b, and after the second it is back on a. Both stops have the coil sides on the horizontal neutral line, where B ≈ 0. There the coil makes no torque and has no EMF induced in it, so it can be shorted and reversed without losing torque or sparking.

Experiment 3
AC in the coil, DC at the supply

Measure how often the coil current reverses, and how long each reversal takes.

Procedure

  1. Watch the three graphs for at least one revolution.
  2. Drag across the graph near ψ = 180°. Find the first and last angles where the + brush reads two segments.

Questions

  1. The real machine runs at 1800 r/min. What is the frequency of the current in the coil?
  2. In a motor, is the commutator acting as a rectifier or an inverter? Explain.
What you should see

The coil current reverses 2 times per revolution; the supply current never reverses. The + brush reads two segments from about ψ = 173° to 187°, so each commutation lasts about 15° (brush width 18° minus the 3° gap). At 1800 r/min a 2-pole coil carries f = P n / 120 = 30 Hz. In a motor DC goes in and AC comes out to the coil, so the commutator is an inverter. In a generator it is a rectifier.

Experiment 4
Reverse the motor

See what changes, and what does not, when the supply is reversed while the motor runs.

Procedure

  1. Let the rotor reach a steady speed and fill in the Before column.
  2. Press ⇄ Reverse current. Watch it brake and spin up again, then fill in After.

Questions

  1. Why does the rotor slow down before it turns the other way?
  2. If you reversed the field and the supply, which way would it turn? Why?
What you should see

Before: CW rotation, • under N, forces CW. After: CCW rotation, × under N, forces CCW. Commutation is still on the neutral line (0° and 180°), so the new direction is just as steady. The rotor’s momentum carries it on briefly while the reversed torque acts as a brake. Reversing both I and B reverses the force twice, so the motor would keep turning the original way.

Experiment 5
The dead spot

Show why a single loop cannot always start, and why more coils fix it.

Procedure

  1. With one loop at ψ = 0°, press Run. Record the torque and whether it starts.
  2. Repeat with the four-coil machine at the same angle.

Questions

  1. Over what range of angles does the single loop make no torque at all? What fraction of a revolution is that?
  2. Why does the four-coil machine start from the same position?
What you should see

One loop: torque 0 and it stays put, so the dead-spot warning appears (use Give it a push). Four coils: torque CW 0.50 and it starts. The single loop is dead whenever both sides are in a neutral zone, ±10° around 0° and 180°: 40° of 360°, about 11% of a revolution. In the four-coil machine, coils 1 and 3 are commutating at ψ = 0°, but coils 2 and 4 are squarely under the poles at full current.

Experiment 6
Torque ripple

Compare how smooth the torque is with one loop and with four coils.

Procedure

  1. Drag across the torque graph and read the Torque readout. Record the largest and smallest values.
  2. Ripple = (max − min) / max × 100%.

Questions

  1. The dashed line on the four-coil torque graph is the followed coil alone. How does it compare with the one-loop torque?
  2. Predict what would happen to the ripple with 16 coils and 16 segments.
What you should see

One loop: max 1.00, min 0, ripple 100%, minimum at 0° and 180°. Four coils: max 1.00, min 0.50, ripple 50%, minimum around 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°, whenever a pair of coils is in the neutral zone. Each coil on its own looks like the one-loop torque at a quarter of the size (half the current, one coil out of four), shifted by 90° from its neighbors. More coils overlap more of these humps, so the ripple keeps shrinking: with 16 coils only a small fraction are ever being commutated at once.

Experiment 7
Two parallel paths

Trace how the supply current divides through a closed lap winding.

Procedure

  1. In the circuit view, read the current label next to each coil at ψ = 315°.
  2. Drag across the graph to ψ = 0° and read them again.

Questions

  1. Which coils form each of the two paths from the + brush to the − brush at ψ = 315°?
  2. Why does each coil carry ½I? At ψ = 0°, which coils are being commutated, and how does the current still reach the − brush?
What you should see

At ψ = 315°: coils 1 and 2 carry +½I, coils 3 and 4 carry −½I. The + brush is on segment a and the − brush on c. One path is a → coil 1 → b → coil 2 → c, and the other is a → coil 4 → d → coil 3 → c (traversed backwards, hence the minus sign). A 2-pole lap winding has a = 2 parallel paths, so each carries I/2. At ψ = 0°, coils 1 and 3 read 0: they are shorted by the brushes and reversing. Coils 2 and 4 still carry ½I each, and the brushes, touching two segments each, carry the current straight past the shorted coils.
