Synchronous Generator Phasor Diagrams

How a changing load reshapes the diagram — operating alone and on an infinite bus

1 · The per-phase equivalent circuit

Each phase of a round-rotor synchronous generator is modeled as an internal generated voltage EA in series with the synchronous reactance XS and the armature resistance RA. What appears at the terminals is the phase voltage Vφ.

EA = Vφ + RAIA + jXSIA
The lecture neglects RA (it is much smaller than XS), giving Vφ = EA − jXSIA. The lab starts with RA = 0 for the same reason — you can switch it on under Machine parameters.

|EA| = KΦω. At constant speed, the magnitude of EA is set only by the field current: more If → more flux → larger |EA|. The lab uses a linear magnetization curve in which If = 1.00 pu gives |EA| equal to rated Vφ. (A real machine saturates, so EA grows more slowly than If at high excitation.)

2 · Drawing the diagram

  1. Vφ is the reference, drawn at 0°.
  2. IA is drawn at the power-factor angle: lagging load → IA below Vφ by θ; leading load → above.
  3. jXSIA starts at the tip of Vφ and is perpendicular to IA (90° ahead of it). If RA is included, RAIA is added first, parallel to IA.
  4. EA closes the triangle from the origin. The angle from Vφ up to EA is the torque (power) angle δ.

In the lab the voltage phasors share one volts scale; IA is drawn to its own amps scale so that only its direction and relative length matter.

3 · Reading power off the diagram

Drop a perpendicular from the tip of EA onto the Vφ axis. Because jXSIA is perpendicular to IA, the angle inside that small triangle is θ, so (with RA = 0):

EA sin δ = XSIA cos θ    ·    EA cos δ − Vφ = XSIA sin θ
Vertical side ∝ real current IAcos θ. Horizontal side ∝ reactive current IAsin θ. Turn on Construction lines in the lab to see both.
P = 3VφIA cos θ = 3VφEA sin δ / XS    ·    Q = 3VφIA sin θ = 3Vφ(EA cos δ − Vφ) / XS
Careful with “∝ P”. The height EA sin δ is proportional to P only while Vφ stays constant (infinite bus). When a generator runs alone, Vφ changes with load, so the height tracks the real current IAcos θ — the power is that current times a Vφ that is itself moving.

4 · Two operating situations

What a load change does depends entirely on what else is connected. Every experiment in this lab is one of these two situations:

Who sets Vφ?The generator itself — Vφ floatsThe power system — Vφ is fixed
Who sets P and Q?The load: P and the power factor are whatever the load demandsThe operator: governor sets P, field current sets Q

Governor (prime mover) controls…FrequencyReal power

Case 1 — Alone: load increases, If and power factor constant

|EA| cannot change, so the tip of EA stays on a circle. |IA| grows at a fixed angle, so jXSIA grows in a fixed direction. The only way to keep the triangle closed is for Vφ to change:

  • Lagging load — Vφ falls significantly.
  • Unity load — Vφ falls slightly.
  • Leading load — Vφ rises (for a sufficiently leading power factor).
VR = (Vnl − Vfl) / Vfl × 100 %
Voltage regulation: large and positive for lagging loads, small and positive at unity, negative for leading loads. At no load IA = 0, so Vnl = EA.

To bring Vφ back, raise the field current: larger |EA| → larger Vφ. This is exactly what an automatic voltage regulator does.

Case 2 — Infinite bus: real power increases, If constant

Opening the governor pushes the rotor ahead: δ increases while |EA| and Vφ stay fixed. The tip of EA swings up its circle, EA sin δ grows in proportion to P, and IA = (EA∠δ − Vφ∠0°)/jXS grows and rotates counter-clockwise. If the machine started over-excited, the power factor moves from lagging, through unity (when EA cos δ = Vφ), to leading. Power cannot exceed the value at δ = 90°:

Pmax = 3VφEA / XS
The static stability limit. Real generators run at 15°–30°, far below it.

Case 3 — Infinite bus: If changes, real power constant

P is fixed by the governor, so EA sin δ and IA cos θ must both stay constant: the tip of EA slides along a horizontal line and the tip of IA slides along a vertical line. Raising If lengthens EA, makes IA more lagging and increases the reactive power supplied. Lowering If does the opposite and eventually makes the generator absorb reactive power.

5 · Excitation and reactive power

Generator is…TestIAReactive power
Over-excitedEA cos δ > Vφlags Vφsupplies Q to the system
Normally excitedEA cos δ = Vφin phase (unity pf)Q = 0
Under-excitedEA cos δ < Vφleads Vφabsorbs Q from the system

The test is on the projection EA cos δ, not on |EA| itself: a heavily loaded generator can have |EA| > Vφ and still be under-excited.

6 · V-curve and capability curve

Plotting |IA| against If at constant P gives a V-curve. The minimum is the unity-power-factor point. For a generator, the right-hand branch (more field) is lagging — supplying Q — and the left-hand branch is leading — absorbing Q. (A synchronous motor is described the other way round, because its current is defined flowing in.)

The capability curve shows the allowed region on the P–Q plane: inside the armature-current limit (a circle of radius 3VφIA,max about the origin) and inside the field-current limit (a circle of radius 3VφEA,max/XS centered at Q = −3Vφ²/XS). In the lab, EA,max is the value needed at rated current and 0.8 pf lagging, so the two limits meet at the rated point.

7 · Not covered this semester

The lab can also show a synchronous motor (current flows in, EA lags Vφ), a synchronous condenser (a motor with no shaft load used purely for reactive power) and a salient-pole rotor (Xd ≠ Xq). They are tucked under Machine parameters → Beyond this course for the curious.
