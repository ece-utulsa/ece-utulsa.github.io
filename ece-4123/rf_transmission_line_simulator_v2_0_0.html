RF Transmission Line Simulator — In-Tool Guide

0. Quick start

  1. On the Explorer tab, set the characteristic impedance Z₀, the load, the line length ℓ, and the frequency f.
  2. Read off the reflection coefficient Γ, the VSWR, and the input impedance Z_in, and watch the standing-wave plot update as you change them.
  3. Switch to Traveling Waves and press Play. Watch the incident and reflected waves combine into the standing-wave pattern, then toggle loss on to see them attenuate.

1. What this tool does

This simulator analyzes a single uniform transmission line driven at one frequency. You set up a line and a load, and the tool shows you two things: the steady-state result — input impedance, reflection, VSWR, and the standing-wave envelope — and the time-domain picture of the incident and reflected waves that build that envelope. Every number on the screen traces back to a relation explained in §4.

2. The two views

Explorer (steady state). Enter the source, the characteristic impedance Z₀, the load (as either series R + jX or parallel G + jB — switch with the load-mode control), the line length ℓ, the frequency f, and the loss (use the lossless toggle, or set an attenuation α). The tab reports Γ, VSWR, and Z_in along with the full set of derived quantities, plots the standing-wave profiles along the line, and draws the line schematic.

Traveling Waves (time domain). The same scene, shown as motion. Three waves animate: the incident v⁺ traveling toward the load, the reflected v⁻ traveling back toward the source, and their sum, the resultant — together with the stationary |V| envelope they trace out. The controls are Play / Pause, Speed, Reset phase, and the loss toggle.

3. How to read the display

  • Four curves, told apart by line weight and pattern so they stay legible in grayscale and on a projector: the resultant is the heaviest line, near-white and solid; the incident wave is teal and solid; the reflected wave is red and dashed; the envelope is steel-grey and dotted.
  • The axis runs load-to-source. Position is the distance z from the load: z = 0 is at the load (right side); the source is at z = ℓ (left side). Every distance quoted below is measured from the load. One caution if you are cross-referencing a textbook: many texts let z run negative back toward the source, whereas here z is always the positive distance from the load.
  • The envelope does not move. It marks the largest value the resultant reaches at each point over a full cycle. The incident and reflected curves travel; the pattern they sum to stands still — which is exactly what "standing wave" means.

4. The equations, explained

Each relation is stated, explained, and tied to where it appears on screen.

4.1 Reflection coefficient

Γ = (Z_L − Z₀) / (Z_L + Z₀)

Γ measures how badly the load is mismatched to the line. If the load matches the line (Z_L = Z₀), there is nothing to reflect, so Γ = 0. A passive load always gives |Γ| ≤ 1. Γ is complex: its magnitude sets how large the reflected wave is, and its angle ∠Γ sets where the standing-wave pattern sits relative to the load. On screen, |Γ| shows up as the height of the reflected (red) wave against the incident (teal) one.

4.2 Standing-wave ratio

VSWR = (1 + |Γ|) / (1 − |Γ|)

As the incident and reflected waves add and subtract along the line, the resultant amplitude swings between a maximum of |V⁺|(1 + |Γ|) and a minimum of |V⁺|(1 − |Γ|). VSWR is the ratio of those two — the tallest point of the envelope over the shortest. VSWR = 1 is a flat envelope (matched); VSWR → ∞ means the minimum falls to zero (total reflection). You read it straight off the envelope.

4.3 Wavelength and the half-wavelength ruler

β = 2π / λ = ω / v_p

β is the phase constant — the radians of phase the wave gains per meter. The standing-wave pattern repeats every half wavelength: adjacent maxima (and adjacent minima) are spaced λ/2 apart, not λ. Raising the frequency f shrinks λ, so more ripples fit on a line of fixed length.

4.4 The standing-wave envelope

|V(z)| = |V⁺| · |1 + Γ·e^(−2jβz)|, ranging over |V⁺|(1 ± |Γ|)

This is the curve the dotted envelope draws. At each distance z from the load, the incident and reflected phasors add with a relative phase of 2βz; as z increases they rotate in and out of alignment, producing the maxima and minima. That factor of −2βz is why the pattern repeats every λ/2.

4.5 Input impedance

Z_in = Z₀ · (Z_L + Z₀·tanh γℓ) / (Z₀ + Z_L·tanh γℓ) — lossless: tanh γℓ → j·tan βℓ

This is what the source actually sees looking into the line — generally not Z_L, because the line transforms the load impedance with distance. Z_in is periodic in ℓ with period λ/2, the same ruler as the standing wave. It tells you whether the source is matched, which is a different question from whether the load is matched.

4.6 Loss and the round trip

γ = α + jβ and |Γ_in| = |Γ_L| · e^(−2αℓ)

With loss, the propagation constant gains a real part α, and each wave decays as it travels: the incident wave is largest at the source and smallest at the load, while the reflected wave is largest at the load and smallest by the time it returns to the source. A reflection makes a round trip of 2ℓ and comes back reduced by e^(−2αℓ). On a long or lossy line this drives the returning wave toward zero — the input looks matched even though the load is not, and the envelope flattens toward the source. An infinite line is the limiting case: nothing returns, and Z_in = Z₀.

6. Try this (guided exercises)

  1. Make the reflection vanish. Set Z_L = Z₀. The red wave disappears and the envelope goes flat — a matched line.
  2. Find the node. Set Z_L = 0 (a short). Find where the envelope touches zero: it sits right at the load. Note the antinode a quarter-wavelength away.
  3. Flip it. Set Z_L very large (an open). The node and antinode swap — now there is an antinode at the load.
  4. Shift the pattern. Set a complex load (try 75 + j25 Ω) and watch the first minimum move off the load. Change X and watch it slide.
  5. Kill the reflection with distance. Turn loss on and lengthen the line. The reflected wave shrinks on its way back and the envelope flattens toward the source — a long line "looks matched."
  6. Change the ruler. Raise the frequency. More ripples appear on the same line as λ shrinks; confirm the maxima stay λ/2 apart.

7. What to notice (common misconceptions)

  • The standing wave doesn't travel. It is the sum of two waves that do; nothing propagates along the envelope itself.
  • VSWR is not |Γ|. It is built from |Γ|, but it expresses the envelope's max/min ratio — even a small |Γ| gives VSWR > 1.
  • A line can look matched without being matched. Loss or length can drive the input reflection near zero while the load stays badly mismatched.
  • |Γ| ≤ 1 holds only for passive loads. Active or negative-resistance loads can exceed it — and those are outside this tool's scope (§9).
  • Matched is not the same as resonant. Matching is about the impedance the source sees, not about a resonant length.

8. How the views connect

The two tabs share one scene: edit a value on either tab and both update. Γ, VSWR, and the envelope are identical across Explorer and Traveling Waves by construction — the envelope on the Traveling-Waves tab is computed by the same engine routine the Explorer uses, and the maximum the resultant reaches over time at each point is exactly that envelope. The time picture and the steady-state picture are two views of one calculation.

9. Model scope and assumptions

This tool models a single uniform transmission line, driven by one sinusoidal source at one frequency (a phasor, or time-harmonic, analysis), carrying a TEM wave with a real characteristic impedance Z₀. Loss is uniform and distributed (γ = α + jβ), and the load is linear and passive. It does not model: cascaded line sections, connectors or discontinuities, radiation, frequency dispersion beyond the entered parameters, nonlinear or active loads, or multi-frequency / transient excitation. Within those bounds, the displayed numbers are exact.

10. Worked example

Take Z₀ = 50 Ω and a load Z_L = 75 + j25 Ω.

Γ = (75 + j25 − 50)/(75 + j25 + 50) = (25 + j25)/(125 + j25)
|Γ| = |25 + j25| / |125 + j25| = 35.36 / 127.48 ≈ 0.277, ∠Γ ≈ +33.7°
VSWR = (1 + 0.277)/(1 − 0.277) ≈ 1.77

Enter this load in the Explorer and confirm |Γ| ≈ 0.28 and VSWR ≈ 1.77. Pick a length — say ℓ = λ/8 — and read Z_in. Then switch to Traveling Waves and confirm that the first minimum sits off the load: because ∠Γ is neither 0° nor 180°, the pattern shifts, unlike the short and open cases.
