CNC Machining Center

Real parts start as a drawing and end as metal chips on the shop floor. In between, a machinist plans every cut, and a computer-controlled mill carries the plan out. Meet the machine, read a part drawing, plan the machining steps, watch your part being cut in 3D, then measure it to see if it passes.

Learning objectives

By the end, students can:

  1. Describe how a part goes from a drawing (CAD) to machining steps (CAM) to G-code to a CNC machine.
  2. Locate a point with X, Y and Z machine coordinates and a work zero.
  3. Read a simple part drawing: sizes, hole callouts and tolerances.
  4. Choose and order machining operations and tools for a part, and explain why the order matters.
  5. Explain the trade-off between cutting faster and breaking tools, and inspect a part against its tolerances.

The activities

  • §01 Meet the machine: a 3D CNC mill. Jog the axes to target points, meet the tools, 3-question check.
  • §02 Read the drawing: pick one of four parts and a material; read its drawing and dimension table.
  • §03 Plan the job: build the list of operations (face, spot drill, drill, pocket, profile), pick tools, depths and feeds; see each toolpath in 3D.
  • §04 Machine it: cycle start; watch the metal come off, the G-code line by line, the tool load.
  • §05 Inspect: every dimension measured against the drawing's tolerance; stars and cycle time.
  • §06 Challenges: every part in every material, beat the target time.
  • §07 Design a part (extension): draw your own part, then plan and machine it.

Key terms

CAD / CAM / CNC
Computer-aided design (the drawing) / computer-aided manufacturing (planning the cuts) / computer numerical control (the machine follows coded instructions).
G-code
The machine's language: one line per move or command, for example G1 X1.25 Y0.5 F30.
Work zero
The point the program measures from: here the front-left corner of the stock (X 0, Y 0) and the finished top of the part (Z 0).
Stock
The block of raw material the part is cut from.
Feed / depth per pass
How fast the tool moves through the material (inches per minute) / how deep it cuts on each trip around.
Tabs
Small bridges left when cutting out the outline so the part stays attached until the end.
Tolerance
How far a measured size may be from the drawing's size and still pass, for example ±0.005 in.

01Meet the machine

A CNC mill holds a spinning cutting tool and moves it in three directions: X (left and right), Y (front and back) and Z (up and down). The block of material, the stock, is clamped in a vise. Every move comes from a program; nobody steers it by hand while it cuts.

1 · CADSomeone draws the part with its sizes and tolerances.
2 · CAMA machinist plans the cuts: which tools, in what order, how deep and how fast.
3 · G-codeThe plan becomes lines of code the machine understands.
4 · CNCThe machine runs the code and cuts the part. Then it is measured.

    03Plan the job

    This is the CAM step: you decide how the machine makes the part. Add operations in the order the machine should do them, and choose the tool, the depth per pass and the feed for each. The tool-load bar shows how hard each cut pushes the tool: past the dashed line it is risky; full red and the tool breaks.

    Machinist's rules of thumb: face the top first so every depth is measured from a flat, clean surface. Spot-drill before you drill so the drill does not wander. Cut the outline (profile) last, and leave tabs so the part does not come loose. A round tool cannot cut a corner sharper than its own radius.
    Plan check
      Estimate

      Click an operation to see its toolpath on the machine below: solid lines are cutting moves, dashed grey lines are fast moves through the air.

      04Machine it

      Press Cycle start. The machine follows your plan, one line of G-code at a time: the line it is running is highlighted in the G-code tab. Watch the chips fly and the tool-load gauge. Feed hold pauses; the speed menu runs the job faster than real time. When it finishes, go to §05 to measure the part.

      Safety on a real machine: doors closed, safety glasses on, hands away while the spindle turns, and one finger near Feed hold on the first run of any new program.

      05Inspect

      Every size on the drawing is measured and compared with its tolerance. A size passes if it is inside the tolerance. Three stars: every size passes and the cycle time beats the target.

      06Challenges

      Make every part, in every material. The example plan is safe but slow: it earns two stars. For three stars, every size must pass and the cycle time must beat the target. Faster feeds and deeper passes save time, but push the tool load too far and the tool breaks. Aluminum is much harder to cut than wax.

        08Exit ticket

        Answer on paper.

        1. Put these in order and say what each does: G-code, CAM, CAD, CNC machine.

          CAD (draw the part) → CAM (plan the cuts) → G-code (the plan as machine instructions) → CNC machine (cuts the part).

        2. Why is the outline (profile) cut last, and what are tabs for?

          Once the outline is cut through, the part is no longer held by the stock, so any later cut would shake or move it. Tabs are small bridges that keep the part attached until the job is done; they are broken and filed off afterwards.

        3. A hole is drawn Ø0.250 ±0.003 THRU. Which drill do you choose, and why spot-drill first?

          The Ø0.250 (1/4 in) drill: a drill makes a hole its own size. A spot drill makes a small cone exactly on the centre so the long drill starts there instead of wandering off position.

        4. You double the feed of a cut. What gets better, and what risk goes up?

          The cycle time goes down (the part is made faster, so it costs less), but the force on the tool goes up; too much and the tool breaks (and the part may be scrapped).
