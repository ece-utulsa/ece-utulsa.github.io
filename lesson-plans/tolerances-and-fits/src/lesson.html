Tolerances, Fits & Stack-ups

No machine makes a part exactly the size on the drawing. So how much can a part be off and still work? Learn to read a tolerance, measure parts like a quality inspector, predict whether two blocks will fit together, and find out why a 3D-printed "0.75-inch tab" won't go into a "0.75-inch slot". Then put it all together on a five-piece puzzle cube.

About 90 minutesAll sizes in inches (in)@@VERSION@@Materials: calipers (0.001 in), a printed slot block and tabs, this page projected

Learning objectives

By the end, students can:

  1. Read a tolerance written three ways and find its largest and smallest allowed sizes.
  2. Decide whether a measured part is in tolerance.
  3. Predict whether two parts fit together with a gap, need a push, or never go together (clearance, transition, interference).
  4. Explain why small errors in several parts add up in an assembly.
  5. Choose a gap to design into 3D-printed parts that must fit together.

The activities

  • §01 Tolerance band: drag the limits on a ruler and watch the same tolerance written three ways. "Match the drawing" gives practice targets.
  • §02 Quality inspector: a 10-part game. Measure each part with the caliper and accept or reject it against its drawing. It keeps score of bad parts shipped and good parts scrapped.
  • §03–04 Fits: the 3D tab-and-slot model, "print a pair and predict", and a diagram where students drag the tab's tolerance zone to make each kind of fit.
  • §05–06 Stack-ups and practice: spacer blocks in a pocket, random builds, and step-by-step practice problems with hints.
  • §07–08 3D printing and the puzzle cube: the printer adds its own tolerance; students tolerance a five-piece cube so it always goes together.

Key terms

Basic size
The size on the drawing that the tolerance is measured from.
Deviation
How far above (+) or below (−) the basic size a part may be.
Limits
The largest and smallest allowed sizes.
Tolerance
Largest allowed size − smallest allowed size. Always positive.
Hole / shaft
The opening, and the part that goes into it (here: the slot and the tab).
MMC
Maximum material condition: the tightest version of a part (smallest hole, largest shaft).
LMC
Least material condition: the loosest version (largest hole, smallest shaft).
Allowance
The gap in the tightest case: smallest hole − largest shaft.

01Reading a tolerance

A drawing gives a basic size and how far a real part may be above or below it. Any size between the two limits is good. The tolerance is the width of that band. The same band can be written three ways.

Unilateral (one way)
0.750+0.004−0.000

Allowed to grow in one direction only. Good parts: 0.750 to 0.754.

Common on holes and slots, so the opening is never smaller than 0.750.

Bilateral (both ways)
0.740 ±0.002
0.740+0.002−0.004

Allowed above and below. Equal (±): 0.738 to 0.742. Unequal: 0.736 to 0.742.

Common when the target is the middle of the band.

Limit
0.754
0.750

Just the two limits, larger on top. No math needed on the shop floor.

Same part as the unilateral example. Tolerance = 0.754 − 0.750 = 0.004.

02Quality inspector

You inspect parts as they come off the machine. For each part: read its drawing, close the caliper jaw on the part to measure it, then Accept it if the size is between the limits or Reject it if not. A size exactly on a limit passes. Ten parts per round.

Work order
Press Start a round.
Debrief. A rejected good part costs the price of one part. An accepted bad part can cost far more: a jammed assembly, a warranty return, a recall. That is why inspectors measure carefully near the limits, and why designers ask for tolerances no tighter than needed.

04Clearance, transition, interference

Whether two parts fit depends on where their two tolerance bands sit. Draw both bands on one scale, measured from the basic size, and you can see the answer. Below, the slot's band is fixed. Drag the tab's band up and down, or drag its top and bottom edges to make it wider or narrower.

    05Stacking tolerances

    Three blocks sit end to end in a pocket. Each block can be a little big or a little small, and every one of those errors ends up in the gap at the end. Too small a gap and the blocks won't fit; too big and they rattle. The worst case puts every part at its worst at the same time: the smallest gap uses the largest blocks in the smallest pocket. Try the buttons, then build random assemblies and count how many work.

    Challenge  RSS (root-sum-square) is a statistics shortcut: errors are random and rarely all go the same way, so the real spread is usually narrower than the worst case. The histogram shows 2,000 random builds.

    06Practice problems

    Work the problem one step at a time. Each answer is checked as you go, with a hint aimed at the mistake you made. After three tries the answer is shown so you can keep going. Answers within ±0.0005 in count.

    Answer key: sample problem

    Part A: slot 0.750 +0.008/−0.000, tab 0.738 ±0.004

    Slot: unilateral; tab: equal bilateral. Slot 0.758 / 0.750; tab 0.742 / 0.734.

    Allowance = 0.750 − 0.742 = 0.008. Max clearance = 0.758 − 0.734 = 0.024. Clearance fit. Fit tolerance 0.016 = 0.008 + 0.008.

    Follow-up: if the tab is modeled at 0.750 and the printer holds ±0.006 on both parts, slot and tab both print 0.744–0.756. Clearance runs −0.012 to +0.012: transition; about half the pairs need force, and undersized printed slots make it worse.

    Part B: blocks 1.000 ±0.004, 0.625 ±0.004, 0.375 ±0.002 in pocket 2.020 +0.008/−0.000, rattle limit 0.028

    Blocks sum 1.990 to 2.010; pocket 2.020 to 2.028. Gapmin = 2.020 − 2.010 = 0.010; gapmax = 2.028 − 1.990 = 0.038.

    RSS (Challenge): mean gap 2.024 − 2.000 = 0.024; ±√(0.004² + 0.004² + 0.004² + 0.002²) = ±0.0072 → 0.017 to 0.031.

    Always fits, but rattles (0.038 > 0.028). Revise the pocket to 2.012 +0.004/−0.000: gap 0.002 to 0.026, meets both under worst case.

    07What this means for 3D printing

    The printer is a tolerance

    A CAD model has no tolerance; the process supplies one. Each printed part lands somewhere inside a band around its modeled size, and mating parts printed on the same machine both carry that band. If you model the tab and slot at the same size, you have designed a transition fit at best.

    • Holes and slots print undersize. Extruded perimeters squish into the opening, and curves are faceted into polygons.
    • First-layer flare ("elephant's foot") widens the bottom of a part and blocks entry into a slot.
    • XY and Z differ. Z is quantized to layer height; XY depends on extrusion width and flow calibration.
    • Shrinkage scales with size and material, so large parts drift more than small ones.

    Design rule: model the clearance in CAD (slot larger than tab), then verify it with a test coupon on your own printer.

    Starting clearances for FDM

    Total gap between slot and tab (both sides combined), modeled in CAD. Common starting points for a calibrated printer with a standard nozzle; resin printers need roughly half.

    Intended fitModeled gap (in)Class
    Press / permanent0.000 – 0.004Interference
    Snug, removable0.006 – 0.010Transition
    Sliding0.012 – 0.016Clearance
    Free / loose0.020 +Clearance

    Typical accuracy: FDM about ±0.004–0.010 in, resin about ±0.002–0.004 in, SLS about ±0.004–0.012 in. Treat as order-of-magnitude; measure your own machine.

    Watch it print

    A 3D printer builds a part one thin layer at a time, squeezing out a bead of melted plastic. Press Play on each view and watch where the plastic ends up compared with the drawing (blue dashed line). Then change the settings.

    What you just saw. (1) A round hole is saved as a shape with flat sides, so the printed hole is smaller than the circle. (2) The bead squishes a little into every opening. (3) The first layer is pressed onto the bed and spreads out, so the bottom of a slot is narrower still, and the bottom of a tab is wider. Fixes: draw the gap in CAD, add a small chamfer on bottom edges, and test on your own printer.

    Printed fit predictor

    Slot modeled at 0.750 in. Set the modeled gap, the printer's accuracy (±, applied to both parts), and how much printed slots run undersize.

    Lab (homework): Print one 0.500 in wide tab and a bar with slots modeled at 0.500, 0.504, 0.508 … 0.524. Record which slot gives a press, snug and sliding fit. Measure five tabs with calipers and estimate your ± accuracy as 3 × standard deviation.

    08Puzzle cube challenge

    Five 3D-printed pieces make a 3 × 3 × 3 cube of 27 small cubes, each 0.750 in on a side (2.250 in overall). Every piece is 4 to 6 small cubes. They drop in from above in order A → E. Wherever a piece drops into, or over, an opening in another piece, there is a joint, and this cube has four. Your job: set the tolerances so the cube always goes together but no joint is sloppy. Assemble it, print random cubes, and see which joint jams. Use "Explode" and "See-through" to look inside.

    Tolerances

    Outer deviations apply to every outside size of a piece (posts, stem, tab). Opening deviations apply to the gap a piece leaves between two of its cubes (A's U, B's top ends, D's and E's arches). Enter signed values in inches.

    Joints

    Answer key: puzzle cube

    The design. 27 cubes of 0.750 in, five pieces of 4 to 6 cubes, dropped in order A → E. Layers from the bottom, back row first: CCC / ABA / AAA, then DCD / BBB / EAE, then DDD / BEB / EEE.

    Joints. Each joint is one cube (0.750 in) between two cubes of another piece, so each is a single slot-and-tab fit. J1: B's stem into A's U. J2: D's arch over C's post. J3: E's arch over A's post. J4: E's tab between B's top ends. B and E each appear twice: once as the opening and once as the part that goes in.

    All basic size: every joint is line-to-line (0.000). It works on paper only; any real variation jams it.

    Hole basis (openings +0.008/−0.000, pieces +0.000/−0.004): every joint 0.000 to +0.012. Always assembles; budget 0.048 of a possible 0.096.

    ±0.004 everywhere: every joint −0.008 to +0.008, a transition fit. Each joint works about half the time, so only about 6% of cubes go together (0.5⁴).

    Hole basis, printed (printer ±0.006, openings print 0.004 undersize): every joint −0.016 to +0.020. About 1 cube in 5 assembles.

    Compensated (openings +0.018/+0.016, pieces +0.000/−0.002, printer ±0.004, 0.004 undersize): every joint +0.004 to +0.024. Always assembles, and the play is right at the 0.024 limit.

    Debrief. A joint's clearance band = opening band + inner band (+ 4 × printer ± when printed). With zero allowance and play exactly at the limit L, each joint allows L of tolerance (L − 4p printed), so every best answer has the same budget, 4L (0.096 in at L = 0.024). The design choice is how to split each joint's L between its two pieces: give the wider tolerance to the feature that is harder to make.

    09Exit ticket

    Answer on paper. Show the largest and smallest sizes you used.

    1. A hole is 0.500 +0.002/−0.000 and a pin is 0.501 ±0.001. Is it a clearance, transition or interference fit?

      Hole 0.500 to 0.502; pin 0.500 to 0.502. Tightest gap 0.500 − 0.502 = −0.002; loosest 0.502 − 0.500 = +0.002. One negative, one positive: transition.

    2. Five blocks, each 0.400 ±0.002, are stacked. What are the longest and the shortest the stack can be?

      Longest 5 × 0.402 = 2.010; shortest 5 × 0.398 = 1.990. The stack's tolerance is 0.020, five times one block's 0.004. (Challenge: RSS gives ±0.002·√5 = ±0.0045.)

    3. Your printer holds ±0.005 in and printed slots come out 0.004 in too small. What gap should you draw between tab and slot so the tab always slides in? (Hint: use the printed fit predictor.)

      Tightest gap = drawn gap − 0.004 − 2 × 0.005 ≥ 0, so draw at least 0.014 in.
